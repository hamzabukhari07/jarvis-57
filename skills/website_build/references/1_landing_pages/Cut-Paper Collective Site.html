HomeApproach

How a rehearsal works.

Four principles keep the work honest. Four moves keep it on schedule.

Four principles

We turn a deliverable-led brief into a question people recognise from their own week, and keep that question visible on the wall from the first day to the last.

What we ask of youOne hour with whoever wrote the brief, and permission to rewrite it.

Paper invites people to move, redraw and disagree. We set up where people already pass: foyers, school halls and depots, never a closed meeting room.

What we ask of youA space people walk through anyway, for at least two days.

We test real routes, timings and routines at the scale the venue allows. A queue is only a queue when people are standing in it.

What we ask of youFive people who will use the place every day, paid for their time.

We close with a kit, the cutting files and a training session. The method should be easy to share, easy to change and impossible to lock away.

What we ask of youSomeone on your team who wants to run the next one.

Four moves, always in this order.

  1. Weeks 1 to 2

    Listen

    Map walks and kitchen-table interviews with the people whose routines will change.

  2. Weeks 3 to 4

    Cut

    A rough version everyone can rearrange, cut from one colour of paper and labelled by hand.

  3. Two public days

    Rehearse

    Run the proposal at full scale, record every change and photograph each version.

  4. Weeks 6 to 8

    Hand over

    Findings, the kit and a training day, so the next decision does not wait for us.

“Nobody defends a plan they helped cut out with their own hands. They improve it.”
Imani Castellane, founder

What a kit contains.

  • Cutting files

    Every wall, table and sign from the rehearsal, ready for a plotter or a craft knife.

  • Floor tape plan

    A one-page layout drawn to scale, so two people can mark out the room in an hour.

  • Facilitation script

    Timed prompts for the day, including what to say when the room goes quiet.

  • Friday photo protocol

    Where to stand and when to shoot, so every version is recorded the same way.

  • Report template

    A twelve-page plain-language structure your team can fill in after each round.

  • Training day

    Six hours with up to twelve staff, ending with a rehearsal they run themselves.

Orchard Commons

A library car park, reimagined as a shared growing space and tested at full size for one weekend.

Client
Quillon Street Library
Year
2025
Format
Exhibition and three workshops
Place
Staff car park, Harbourside
Duration
Nine weeks
Team
Imani, Theo and six local growers

The question

What would Saturday morning feel like if the orchard already existed?

The library had approval in principle for a community orchard on its staff car park. Neighbours had seen the drawings, and most of them still could not picture it. The staff who would lose their parking spaces could picture it very clearly.

What happened

Over one weekend, residents built the orchard at full size: forty paper trees, six raised beds, two paths and a lilac greenhouse, laid directly over the painted parking bays.

Visitors moved the pieces, argued about shade and redrew the path to the bike racks twice. By Sunday the proposal had a shared language. People talked about the pear corner and the quiet bench instead of the lost spaces.

What we made

  • A full-scale paper orchard, assembled and struck in a weekend
  • Three co-design workshops with a printed cutting kit
  • A pledge wall that turned visits into planting-day volunteers
  • A twelve-page plain-language report for the council vote
1,200visitors across the two public days
340planting pledges on the paper wall
4 wksfrom rehearsal to Phase 1 approval

Tomorrow’s Timetable

A new bus network, tested through the journeys people actually make.

Client
Kittering Valley Transit
Year
2024
Format
Two-day futures rehearsal
Place
Eastgate bus depot
Duration
Six weeks
Team
Priya, Theo and four drivers

The question

Could someone make their usual journey without help from the planning team?

Clear maps could not answer every rider’s question about the proposed network, and the consultation period closed in two months. The transit team wanted to know where people would get lost before the network went live, not after.

What happened

We built a paper interchange with nine platforms inside a working depot. Riders followed their real routes while drivers called the services from a timetable board.

The rehearsal surfaced a crossing that looked harmless on the map and a transfer nobody could find. Both changed before the network was published.

What we made

  • A full-size paper interchange with nine platforms
  • Sixty scripted journeys built from anonymised ticket data
  • A network map redrawn with riders during the event
  • A rehearsal kit now used at every depot before a change
60riders walked their own daily journey
3route changes adopted before launch
62%preferred the redrawn map after the day

The Long Lunch

A school lunch hall designed with the students who use it.

Client
Fernhollow Secondary School
Year
2024
Format
Eight-week co-design
Place
Main hall, Fernhollow
Duration
One spring term
Team
Imani, Priya and 24 students

The question

What would make students want to stay for the whole of lunch?

Long queues and a noisy hall were pushing students out of the canteen and onto the high street. The school had a refurbishment budget and one chance to spend it well.

What happened

Students modelled tables, serving points and quieter corners at table-top scale, then worked with the kitchen team on menus that matched the new layout.

A Saturday trial at full scale let the school test the layout with 180 students and the real kitchen before committing to the refurbishment.

What we made

  • A student-led co-design programme over eight sessions
  • A full-scale paper hall for the Saturday trial
  • Three costed menus with the catering team
  • A lunch-hall kit the student council reruns each spring

Signal Garden

A museum landscape made from visitors’ memories of weather.

Client
Saltmarsh Museum of Weather
Year
2023, touring 2025
Format
Participatory installation
Place
Gallery 3, then touring
Duration
Twelve-week build
Team
Theo, Imani and two curators

The question

How do you make a climate gallery people talk in, rather than walk through?

The museum wanted a climate room built around personal memory and shared conversation rather than another wall of charts.

What happened

Visitors cut a shape, write a remembered year on the back and pin it into a growing landscape of clouds, hills and rain.

Every Friday at four, a photograph from the same spot records how the room has changed and which stories have gathered around it. Those photographs became the museum’s evaluation.

What we made

  • A modular paper landscape that packs into two crates
  • A cutting station with twelve weather shapes and a script
  • A Friday photo protocol that doubles as evaluation
  • A touring pack for host museums, with a training day

Method

Start with scissors.

People feel free to move a paper wall. That small permission changes the whole conversation.

Imani Castellane12 June 20265 min read

We first used cardboard because it was cheap. We kept using it because of what it does to a room. Put a rendering of a new library on an easel and people comment on it. Put a paper wall in the middle of the floor and somebody will pick it up and move the entrance.

The social physics of a paper wall

A polished rendering asks for approval. An unfinished object asks for help. When the thing in front of you was obviously made this morning, disagreement stops feeling rude and starts feeling useful.

We have watched a retired engineer and a nine-year-old argue, politely and at length, about where a bench should go. Neither of them would have come to a consultation evening.

Rough on purpose

Early models stay simple on purpose, so the room talks about positions and possibilities before anyone talks about finishes. We hold ourselves to four rules:

  • First versions are cut from one colour of paper.
  • Labels are handwritten, never printed.
  • Nothing is glued down until the second day.
  • Colour and texture come after a shared decision.
“Nobody wants to trample your hours. Give them something with fewer hours in it.”Written on the studio wall

What it costs

This approach asks clients to sit with uncertainty a little longer than they are used to. The first day can look messy, and that is the point. The reward is a plan more people have helped to shape, and fewer people want to fight.

So we start with scissors and let the conversation decide the finish. To see the four rules at work, read how they shaped Orchard Commons.

Field notes

Two days in the depot.

Clipboard notes from a rehearsal of a new bus interchange, written up the evening after.

Theo Marchetti3 April 20266 min read

Kittering Valley Transit gave us a working depot for two days and four drivers who knew every route by heart. These are the notes I kept on the clipboard, lightly tidied.

Thursday, 16:00We mark out the platform edges at full scale with floor tape, so every transfer becomes a walk people can feel in their legs.
Friday, 07:30Cardboard platforms, timetable boards and a mock ticket hall go up. The drivers rearrange the boards before we have finished taping them, which is a good sign.
Friday, 11:00Riders start testing awkward journeys. One transfer exposes a crossing that looked harmless on the map and is genuinely frightening with a pram.
Saturday, 09:00Riders redraw the arrangement on the floor while the planning team watches from the edge of the room, as agreed.
Sunday, 18:00We strike the set in ninety minutes. The depot keeps the boards for staff briefings.

What changed before lunch on Friday

  • Platforms two and six swapped places.
  • The crossing disappeared from the plan entirely.
  • Somebody added a paper bench, and it stayed.
“A planner standing beside the map answers questions the map should answer.”Rehearsal rule number four

What we would do differently

Start the riders earlier. The most useful hour was the first one on Friday, before anyone was tired or polite. Next time the doors open at seven.

The full story, with the numbers, is in the case study: Tomorrow’s Timetable.

Glossary

A studio glossary.

Eight words we use every day, and what we mean when we use them.

Priya Solberg18 February 20264 min read

Every studio grows its own language. Ours has been written on the wall, crossed out and rewritten often enough that it is worth writing down properly.

Bright speculation
Making a possible future concrete enough to explore together, without pretending it is already decided.
The wall
The shared place for the project question, every decision and every change. If it is not on the wall, it has not been agreed.
Rehearsal
A proposal tried at full scale, with the people who will use it, for a limited time.
Rough on purpose
The first version is cut from one colour of paper and labelled by hand, so the room discusses positions, not finishes. See Start with scissors.
The Friday photo
A photograph taken from the same spot at the same time each week, to record how the work changes.
Awkward journeys
The difficult routes that reveal what a typical route misses: the pram, the wheelchair, the late shift.
Handover
The kit and the practice that let the host team carry on without us.
Leave the chalk
Letting the host keep the useful marks, models and ideas a rehearsal leaves behind, instead of tidying them away.
“Not a pilot, which implies commitment, and not a workshop, which implies chairs.”On the word rehearsal

If a word is missing, it probably means we have not needed it yet. Suggestions are welcome through the contact page.

Privacy

What we collect, why, and how to ask us to delete it. Last updated 1 September 2026.

What we collect

When you write to us or join the letter, we keep your name, email address, organisation and the note you sent. We do not use advertising cookies or tracking pixels on this site.

Why we keep it

Only to reply to you, to send the monthly letter if you asked for it, and to keep a record of projects we have discussed. We never sell or share your details.

Rehearsal photographs

We photograph rehearsals to record how a plan changes. Anyone can ask not to be photographed at the door, and we blur faces before publishing unless we have written consent.

Your choices

Every letter has an unsubscribe link. To see or delete what we hold about you, write to the studio and we will reply within thirty days.
